# Who gains, who does not, who loses by staying out

Written 6 August 2026. Lives as the **Who gains** tab in `analysis.html`, second from the left.

Four questions were asked. They get four different kinds of answer, and the difference between
those kinds matters more than any individual number below.

| Question | Status |
|---|---|
| 1. Who is benefitting? | Answerable |
| 2. Who is not? | Answerable |
| 3. Who loses out by not being a member? | **Not answerable** — needs a counterfactual this design cannot supply |
| 4. Who would be better off outside? | **Not empirical** — a question about what to value |

## A change of yardstick, and why it was necessary

Everywhere else in this project convergence is measured as a share of the EU average. That
denominator moves: as the East catches up the EU average rises, so a rich member's line falls
even while its economy grows. It answers *is the Union converging*. It cannot answer *is this
country gaining*, which is what all four questions ask.

Every figure here therefore measures each country against a **fixed external benchmark — US GNI
per capita at PPP** — over one common window, 2000 to 2025. No event-time alignment, no moving
denominator, members and non-members held to the same twenty-five years.

This buys a like-for-like comparison and a testable placebo. It does **not** buy causality. It is
a fixed-window difference between groups of countries, not the event study the rest of the
project runs, and the window contains the accession run-up as well as membership itself.

## 1. Who is benefitting

Three tiers, strongest evidence first.

**Accounting fact — the budget.** Not an estimate. The Commission publishes what each member
paid and what was spent in it. On the broad convention (stripping administration, counting
customs as national revenue), average annual net position as a share of national income since
accession:

| Receiving most | %GNI/yr | Cumulative | Paying most | %GNI/yr | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lithuania | 3.11 | €24.5bn | Germany | −0.49 | −€366.9bn |
| Hungary | 2.50 | €67.7bn | France | −0.31 | −€170.1bn |
| Latvia | 2.49 | €14.6bn | United Kingdom | −0.36 | −€160.3bn |
| Estonia | 2.38 | €10.5bn | Italy | −0.29 | −€120.8bn |
| Bulgaria | 2.33 | €25.4bn | Netherlands | −0.71 | −€119.4bn |
| Greece | 2.20 | €104.1bn | Belgium | −0.72 | −€69.5bn |
| Romania | 1.76 | €58.7bn | Sweden | −0.44 | −€45.8bn |
| Poland | 1.62 | €167.7bn | Denmark | −0.37 | −€25.7bn |

Note the asymmetry of scale: recipients take several percent of national income a year;
contributors pay well under one percent of theirs.

**Causally established — trade.** Trade openness in the East, **+20.5pp of GDP**, 11 of 11
countries positive, 95% interval +14.3 to +26.7, pre-accession placebo +0.6. The strongest
result in the study. FDI into Western members **+1.0pp of GDP**, small but surviving every check.

**Descriptive, and it survives its placebo — income.** Over 2000–2025 the eleven Eastern members
gained a median **+29.4 points** on the US level; the five Western Balkan non-members gained
**+18.1**. Gap **+10.3pp**, 95% interval **+5.1 to +15.5**, excluding zero. Run entirely over
pre-accession years (1997–2000) the same comparison reports **+0.1** — the groups were not
already diverging.

The obvious objection, that poorer countries catch up faster anyway, was tested rather than
assumed. Within the Eastern region starting income explains **1%** of the variation in
subsequent gain, and it runs the wrong way for the objection: the members started *richer* (mean
28.4% of US level against 14.9%) and gained more regardless. Ranked by residual from the fitted
catch-up line, the top seven are all members and three of the bottom four are not.

The overlap is real and should be stated: Slovenia (−6.1) and Czechia (−2.7) sit below
Montenegro (−3.0) and Albania (−5.9). This is a difference in group medians, not a rule that
holds country by country.

## 2. Who is not benefitting

**Members that lost ground on the US over twenty-five years:** Luxembourg −13.4, Italy −3.2,
Greece −2.9, United Kingdom −0.8. Barely moving: France +0.9, Finland +1.8, Sweden +4.5.
(Luxembourg's figure is a profit-shifting artefact and should not be read as a decline in
living standards.)

**Greece is the sharpest case in the dataset.** It received 2.2% of national income a year from
the budget across the whole period — the fourth-highest rate in the Union — and still ended
2025 below where it started relative to the US. Whatever the budget bought there, it was not
convergence.

**One measurable cost of integration, and it is the only one in this study.** The 2012 downturn
was European, not global: **61% of members contracted against 23% of non-members**, where 2009
(96% / 77%) and 2020 (93% / 92%) hit both alike. Deeper integration transmits shocks. That is a
real, quantified debit against membership and it belongs on the ledger.

**Eastern FDI is negative.** −3.1pp of GDP against the Balkan controls, surviving its placebo.
Treat it as real but narrow — the comparators were privatising state assets from very low bases
during exactly those years.

## 3. Who loses out by not being a member

**This question has no answer here, and the reason is structural rather than a data gap.** It
requires knowing what Norway, Switzerland or Serbia would look like *inside* the Union. That is
a counterfactual about countries that never entered, and no comparison of observed outcomes can
produce it. The non-members are not a random sample: Norway holds sovereign wealth no member
has, Switzerland has bilateral market access without membership, and the Western Balkans carry a
war the members did not fight.

What can be said is narrower. The Western Balkan countries gained a median +18.1 points on the
US level while their Eastern member neighbours gained +29.4, and starting income does not
account for the difference. Whether membership *caused* that, or whether the same institutional
qualities that made a country eligible also made it grow, this design cannot separate.
**Accession was never random**, and that limitation is permanent.

**For the wealthy non-members the answer is cleaner, and it is negative: no measurable penalty.**
Western members gained a median +5.8 points against Norway, Switzerland and Iceland's +13.6 —
but the 95% interval on that difference runs −17.9 to +5.9, spanning zero, and the pre-accession
placebo already reports +2.9 in the members' favour. Three comparators cannot settle it. What is
not in doubt is the level: Norway and Switzerland sit above every EU member on income per head
except Ireland and Luxembourg, and both of those are statistical artefacts.

## 4. Who would be better off outside

**Not a question this study can answer**, and not because of a data limitation. It asks for a
judgment about what to value. What the data can do is set out the terms of the trade honestly.

| On the ledger for membership | On the ledger against |
|---|---|
| Trade openness +20.5pp of GDP in the East, 11 of 11, placebo +0.6 | The 2012 downturn was European, not global: 61% of members contracted against 23% of non-members |
| Eastern members closed +11.3pp more on US income than their non-member neighbours; pre-trend does not explain it | Greece lost ground while receiving 2.2% of national income a year. Italy lost 3.2 points |
| Receipts of 2–3% of national income a year for poorer members, sustained two decades | Norway and Switzerland are richer per head than every member bar two artefacts |
| FDI into Western members +1.0pp of GDP, surviving every check | Germany pays €367bn net, France €170bn, on the broad convention |

**The one country that has actually run the experiment is the United Kingdom, and this project
could not identify the effect.** The estimate swings from −7 to −21 percentage points depending
on which comparators are chosen, and both routes fail the project's own checks. That is the most
useful thing to know about question 4: the single real test of it produced a number nobody
should quote.

The ledger is also incomplete. It prices what this dataset measures — income, trade, investment,
budget flows, shock transmission. It does not price sovereignty, freedom of movement, regulatory
autonomy, security guarantees, or the value people place on any of them. Those are most of what
the argument is actually about.

## Two specifications disagree, and both are reported

The Findings tab says the income effect is not demonstrable. This tab reports +10.3 points. Both
are in the data and neither is a mistake.

The **event study** compares t+6..t+10 against t−5..t−1. For the 2004 wave that is 2010–2014
against 1999–2003: it isolates the accession *step*, stops a decade short of today, and its
pre-window sits inside the accession negotiations, so part of the treatment is already in the
baseline. Netting its own placebo off leaves **+2.5 points** — same sign, not significant.

The **calendar comparison** here spans the whole period, run-up included, and runs to 2025. The
reconciliation is that most of the Eastern gain is spread across the accession process and the
years after the event study stops looking — which is what you would expect if the mechanism is
trade and investment integration built up over decades rather than a discontinuity on the day of
entry.

Where two defensible specifications disagree, this project reports both rather than choosing the
more flattering one. The event study remains the stricter test, and trade remains the only
outcome that clears it unambiguously.
