# Five questions about accession

Written 6 August 2026, three weeks before Iceland's advisory referendum of 29 August, in answer to
five questions circulating in the Icelandic debate. Every factual claim carries a credited source.
Where the honest answer is that a thing cannot be established to the study's confidence bar, that is
said rather than papered over.

Three of the five questions have clean legal answers. One — sovereignty — is partly a question about
values, and the most useful thing this document can do is set out exactly what is transferred, so
that the argument is about something real. One — purchasing power — has a clean empirical answer for
Iceland specifically, and it is not the answer the question expects.

---

## The referendum itself

Althingi document 1245 of the 157th legislative session, adopted **28 May 2026**, provides for an
**advisory** referendum (*ráðgefandi þjóðaratkvæðagreiðsla*) on **29 August 2026**. The question, in
full:

> **Á Ísland að hefja á ný aðildarviðræður við Evrópusambandið?** ❏ Já. ❏ Nei.
>
> *Should Iceland resume accession negotiations with the European Union? Yes / No.*

Two things follow from the wording. The vote is about **resuming negotiations**, not about joining.
And the result is advisory: nothing in the resolution commits the Althingi to act on it, and any
accession treaty would be a separate decision.

Source: [Althingi, þskj. 1245, 157. löggjafarþing](https://www.althingi.is/altext/157/s/1245.html)

## 1. Have members given up their sovereignty by joining?

**Powers have been transferred, and the Court of Justice has said so in those words. Whether that
amounts to "giving up sovereignty" is a judgement about how much, in which areas, and against what
alternative — and the study can inform that judgement without settling it.**

### What the Court said

In **Costa v ENEL (Case 6/64, 15 July 1964)** the Court held that the founding treaty created "its
own legal system which… became an integral part of the legal systems of the member states", and that
"the transfer by the states from their domestic legal system to the Community legal system of the
rights and obligations arising under the Treaty carries with it **a permanent limitation of their
sovereign rights**". In **Internationale Handelsgesellschaft (11/70, 17 December 1970)** the Court
extended this against national *constitutional* law: the validity of a Union measure "cannot be
affected by allegations that it runs counter to either fundamental rights as formulated by the
constitution of that State or the principles of a national constitutional structure". A year earlier,
**Van Gend en Loos (26/62, 5 February 1963)** had established direct effect — individuals can rely on
Union law in their own national courts without their government's intermediation.

A detail worth knowing, because it cuts both ways in the argument: **primacy is not written into the
Treaties.** It sits in **Declaration 17** annexed to the Final Act of the intergovernmental
conference that adopted the Lisbon Treaty, which merely "recalls that, in accordance with well
settled case law of the Court of Justice…, the Treaties and the law adopted by the Union on the
basis of the Treaties have primacy over the law of Member States". Article 51 TEU gives treaty rank
to Protocols and Annexes; declarations are not mentioned. So primacy is a judge-made doctrine the
member states acknowledged politically rather than enacted. An express primacy clause existed in the
2004 Constitutional Treaty and was dropped when that treaty failed.

### What is actually transferred — the list that matters

**Exclusive Union competence (Article 3(1) TFEU) — five areas, and only five:** the customs union;
the competition rules necessary for the internal market; monetary policy for the states whose
currency is the euro; **the conservation of marine biological resources under the common fisheries
policy**; and the common commercial policy. In these, only the Union may legislate.

**Shared competence (Article 4(2) TFEU) — eleven areas:** internal market; social policy for the
aspects defined in the Treaty; economic, social and territorial cohesion; agriculture and fisheries
excluding conservation; environment; consumer protection; transport; trans-European networks;
energy; the area of freedom, security and justice; and common safety concerns in public health.
Shared competence is *pre-emptible*: member states may act "to the extent that the Union has not
exercised its competence".

**Supporting competence only (Article 6 TFEU) — seven areas** where Union acts "shall not entail
harmonisation": health protection, industry, culture, tourism, education and youth and sport, civil
protection, administrative cooperation.

Against this stand **Article 5(1)–(2) TEU** — "Competences not conferred upon the Union in the
Treaties remain with the Member States" — and **Article 4(2) TEU**, which obliges the Union to
respect national identities "inherent in their fundamental structures, political and
constitutional", and states that "**national security remains the sole responsibility of each
Member State**".

### The mechanism that does the work

Not primacy in the abstract — **qualified majority voting**. The Council's own figure is that "about
80% of all EU legislation" is adopted by QMV. The threshold is 55% of member states (15 of 27)
representing 65% of the EU population; a blocking minority must include at least four states. A
member state can be outvoted and remain bound. That is the operative transfer.

Unanimity survives in, among others: foreign and security policy; citizenship rights; enlargement;
harmonisation of indirect taxation; own resources and the multiannual financial framework; parts of
justice and home affairs (the European prosecutor, family law, operational police cooperation); and
social security and social protection harmonisation. Treaty revision (Art 48 TEU) and accession
(Art 49 TEU) require unanimity plus ratification by every member state.

### It is reversible, and that has been demonstrated

**Article 50 TEU**: "Any Member State may decide to withdraw from the Union in accordance with its
own constitutional requirements." The *decision* is unilateral — the Treaties cease to apply two
years after notification whether or not an agreement is reached. What is not unilateral is the terms
of exit, or an extension. The United Kingdom notified on 29 March 2017 and left on 31 January 2020.
In **Wightman (C-621/18, 10 December 2018)** the Court held that a notifying state may also
**revoke** the notification unilaterally, on terms unchanged.

### National courts have pushed back, and the pushback has not been resolved

- **German Federal Constitutional Court, PSPP, 5 May 2020 (2 BvR 859/15 and joined cases).** Held
  the ECB's bond-buying decisions *ultra vires*, and — for the first time — held that a judgment of
  the Court of Justice was itself rendered ultra vires. The Commission opened infringement
  proceedings on 9 June 2021 and **closed them on 2 December 2021** after the German federal
  government formally affirmed the primacy, autonomy and effectiveness of Union law and undertook to
  avoid a repetition. It ended with an undertaking from the government, not with the Court resiling
  from its doctrine — which remains asserted.
- **Danish Supreme Court, Ajos, 6 December 2016 (Case 15/2014).** Declined to follow a preliminary
  ruling it had itself requested, holding there was "no basis for the proposition that Denmark has
  transferred to the EU Court of Justice… the power to order Danish courts to disapply clear Danish
  legislation" on the basis of an unwritten general principle.
- **Polish Constitutional Tribunal, K 3/21, 7 October 2021.** Held provisions of the TEU themselves,
  as interpreted, incompatible with the Polish Constitution. The furthest-reaching of the three.

### How much national law comes from the EU

**No reliable single figure exists, and the best-credited source says so.** The House of Commons
Library's *How much legislation comes from Europe?* (Research Paper 10/62, 13 October 2010) found
that between 1997 and 2009, 6.8% of UK primary legislation and 14.1% of statutory instruments had a
role in implementing EU obligations; that counting directly applicable regulations makes it possible
"to justify any measure between 15% and 50% or thereabouts"; and concluded that "there is no totally
accurate, rational or useful way of calculating the percentage of national laws based on or
influenced by the EU". Its survey of other member states found published estimates ranging from
about 6% to 84% depending entirely on method. The share is real, high in some sectors (environment,
agriculture, customs, product regulation) and near zero in others (defence, direct taxation,
criminal law, family law). Any headline percentage should be treated as a methodological artefact.

### The Icelandic comparison the debate usually omits

Iceland already applies a large part of this body of law through the EEA — **15 of the 25 policy
areas in this study's coverage map apply in full**, and the decisive row in that map is "a vote on
the rules", where Iceland scores *none*. EEA states adopt single-market law with no seat in the
Council and no members of the European Parliament. There is consultation at the drafting stage and a
formal right of reservation that has never been used to block an act outright. So the sovereignty
question at the referendum is not *rules or no rules*; it is **whether to acquire a vote on rules
Iceland largely already takes, at the price of extending them to fisheries, agriculture, trade
policy and the customs union.**

## 2. Can the European Parliament pass referendums that change member states' agreements — could the EU force Iceland onto the European power grid?

**No, on both halves. The premise of the first half does not exist, and the second is expressly
disclaimed in writing by the Commission.**

### The Parliament cannot hold a referendum

There is no EU-level referendum mechanism of any kind. The European Parliament legislates jointly
with the Council under the ordinary legislative procedure; it does not put questions to voters.
Referendums in the European Union are national instruments held under national constitutional law.
The nearest thing the Treaties contain is the European Citizens' Initiative, which can only invite
the Commission to propose legislation.

Nor can ordinary legislation rewrite a member state's accession terms. Those terms are in the
accession treaty — primary law. Changing them requires Treaty revision under Article 48 TEU:
unanimity plus ratification by every member state in accordance with its own constitutional
requirements.

### On the grid specifically, four independent barriers

**Article 194(1)(d) TFEU** sets the Union's objective as to "**promote** the interconnection of
energy networks". Promote, not compel. And **Article 194(2), second subparagraph** carves out
exactly the subject matter in question:

> "Such measures shall not affect a Member State's right to determine the conditions for exploiting
> its energy resources, its choice between different energy sources and the general structure of its
> energy supply."

**The 15% interconnection target is indicative, not binding.** It originates in a European Council
conclusion of October 2014, and its legal home is Article 4(d)(1) of Regulation (EU) 2018/1999,
which obliges a member state to set out in its national energy and climate plan "the level of
electricity interconnectivity that the Member State **aims for** in 2030 **in consideration of** the
electricity interconnection target for 2030 of at least 15%". The binding obligation created is a
duty to *state a figure in a plan*. There is no deadline, no sanction and no obligation to build.

**The TEN-E Regulation (EU) 2022/869 confers no construction duty — and gives the member state a
veto over listing in the first place.** Article 3(3): "each individual proposal for a project shall
require the approval of the Member States to whose territory the project relates." Article 7's
priority status is expressly conditional on national law already providing for such categories.
Article 5(7)'s escalation mechanism bites on a *project promoter* that is failing to deliver a
project the member state has already approved, and its state-facing verbs are permissive.

**Energy solidarity does not fill the gap.** In **Germany v Poland (C-848/19 P, Grand Chamber, 15
July 2021)** the Court held that the solidarity principle in Article 194(1) is justiciable and
produces binding legal effects. But its operative content is a duty to take other parties' interests
into account when adopting measures. It is a duty of consideration, allied to sincere cooperation —
not a positive duty to build anything.

### For Iceland the question is doubly moot

None of the instruments in this debate is EEA law. **Regulation 2018/1999** (the 15% target),
**Regulation 2022/869** (TEN-E), **Directive (EU) 2019/944** and **Regulation (EU) 2019/943** have
none of them been incorporated into the EEA Agreement by the EEA Joint Committee; EEA Annex IV still
carries the Third Energy Package. And Iceland holds a consensus veto over future incorporation under
**EEA Article 93(2)**, though refusal is not costless: Article 102 provides for provisional
suspension of the affected part of the Annex if the Joint Committee cannot agree within six months.

Most directly, the **Joint Understanding agreed between the European Commission and Iceland on 22
March 2019** states in terms:

> "The Icelandic electricity system is currently an isolated system and it is not connected with an
> interconnector between Iceland and the EU's internal energy market… large parts of the provisions
> of the Third Energy Package, namely those that concern cross-border exchanges and infrastructure
> in electricity, do not apply or have no practical relevance to Iceland in the absence of any
> interconnection… **Decisions on electricity interconnectors between Iceland and the EU's internal
> electricity market lie entirely within the competence of Icelandic authorities.**"

### The Norwegian precedent says the same thing

This is the most contested case in Europe of an EFTA state ceding energy-regulatory authority. On 22
March 2018 the Storting consented to taking the Third Energy Package into the EEA Agreement, placing
the Norwegian regulator under ACER for cross-border questions. Opponents argued this required a
three-quarters constitutional majority. On **31 October 2023** the Supreme Court of Norway, sitting
in plenary, rejected the challenge (**HR-2023-2030-P**) and held that a simple majority sufficed
because the transfer was of limited significance. The judgment says of ACER's powers:

> "Det er videre klart at artikkel 8 ikke gir myndighet til å fastsette strømpriser eller treffe
> beslutninger om å bygge nye utenlandskabler eller kraftverk…"
>
> *It is furthermore clear that Article 8 does not confer authority to set electricity prices or to
> take decisions on building new foreign cables or power plants…*

And structurally, ACER's decisions do not reach Norway directly at all: in the two-pillar EEA system
the parallel decision is taken by the EFTA Surveillance Authority, and formally binds only the
Norwegian regulator.

### The empirical demonstration

The **IceLink** submarine cable to the United Kingdom was explored from 2012 and held EU
project-of-common-interest status. It was never built. Nothing followed — no infringement, no
penalty, no direction to proceed. It does not appear on the fourth Union list of 2020.

### The honest qualification

Accession would change Iceland's legal position: as a member, Regulation 2018/1999 and the TEN-E
Regulation would apply. **But neither compels construction even for a member state**, Article 194(2)
protects a member state's choice about the structure of its energy supply, and TEN-E Article 3(3)
requires the member state's own approval before a project can be listed. The answer is the same
inside the Union as outside it. What *would* change is that Iceland's electricity market rules would
be set by instruments Iceland could no longer veto — but a rule about how a market operates is not a
power to lay a cable.

## 3. Is there a central interest rate members use, and would it take over Iceland's?

**Yes, there is one — but only for the states that have adopted the euro, and adoption is a separate
decision whose timing Iceland would control in practice.**

### The position today

The ECB sets policy rates for the euro area, which since **1 January 2026** comprises **21 of the 27
member states**. Bulgaria adopted the euro on that date under Council Decision (EU) 2025/1407 of 8
July 2025, at the unchanged ERM II central rate of EUR 1 = BGN 1.95583. Six member states remain
outside: **Denmark**, which holds the sole opt-out (Protocol No 16, from Maastricht and the
Edinburgh European Council of December 1992), and **Czechia, Hungary, Poland, Romania and Sweden**,
which hold derogations.

As of August 2026 the ECB's deposit facility rate is **2.25%**, the main refinancing rate 2.40% and
the marginal lending facility 2.65%, following a 25 basis point increase decided on 11 June 2026 and
held at the meeting of 23 July. The Central Bank of Iceland's seven-day term deposit rate is
**7.75%**, raised by 25 basis points on 20 May 2026 — a spread of **550 basis points**.

### What accession would commit Iceland to

The Commission's own formulation: "**All EU Member States, except Denmark, are required to adopt the
euro**" and "**The Treaty does not specify a particular timetable**." No state acceding since
Maastricht has been granted an opt-out, and there is no reason to expect Iceland would be the first.

But the commitment runs through the convergence criteria of Article 140 TFEU and Protocol No 13, one
of which is **at least two years' participation in ERM II without severe tensions**. Entering ERM II
is a voluntary act with no Treaty deadline. **Sweden is the demonstration**: a member since 1995,
legally committed to the euro, never in ERM II. The Commission's 2026 Convergence Report records
that "the Swedish krona does not participate in the Exchange Rate Mechanism II. Sweden operates a
free-floating exchange rate regime." Thirty-one years, no euro, no sanction.

So the precise answer is: **membership would commit Iceland in principle and set no date. The ECB
rate would replace the Icelandic rate only on euro adoption, which cannot happen without Iceland
first choosing to enter ERM II.**

### What would actually change, and a measurement warning

The substantive question is not the level of the rate but the loss of the exchange rate as an
adjustment mechanism. That argument has been made from inside the Central Bank of Iceland in both
directions — see Deputy Governor Arnór Sighvatsson's 2012 lecture, which argued that króna
volatility has acted as a source of shocks rather than an absorber of them and that ERM II
membership would reduce Iceland's foreign reserve needs. This document takes no position on the
merits.

**A measurement trap worth flagging**, because it appears constantly in the Icelandic debate. In
July 2026 Iceland's headline CPI inflation was **5.3%**; the euro area's HICP was **2.9%**. These are
not comparable. The Icelandic CPI includes owner-occupied housing cost; the HICP does not. On the
harmonised basis, Iceland's HICP in June 2026 was **2.9%** against a euro-area 2.9%. Any argument
built on the headline gap is measuring housing, not inflation.

## 4. Is purchasing power greater in the EU?

**Not for Iceland. Iceland is already above the EU average on every measure, and would rank in the
top five of the Union on both of them. But Icelandic prices are the highest in Europe, and that is
where the real question lies.**

### Two measures, both from credited sources

From this study's own data store, **gross national income per capita at purchasing power parity**
for 2025 (World Bank): Iceland **83,250** international dollars against an EU aggregate of
**65,147** — Iceland **27.8% above** the EU average. In 2000 the ratio was 1.33; in 2025 it is 1.28.
Iceland's lead has narrowed slightly over twenty-five years, not widened.

From **Eurostat**, for reference year 2025 (published 17 June 2026, provisional), on the index where
EU27 = 100 and cross-checked against Statistics Iceland's own release of 19 June 2026:

| Measure, EU27 = 100 | Iceland | Norway | Switzerland | Highest EU state |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Actual individual consumption per capita, PPS | **115** | 128 | 114 | Luxembourg 145 |
| GDP per capita, PPS | **128** | 159 | 157 | Luxembourg 239 |
| Price level index, actual individual consumption | **183.7** | 138.4 | 181.0 | Luxembourg 148.0 |
| Price level index, food and non-alcoholic beverages | **155.8** | 133.1 | 155.9 | Luxembourg 122.4 |

Actual individual consumption per capita in purchasing power standards is the measure closest to
what a household can actually buy. On it, Iceland at 115 would rank **fourth** in the Union, behind
Luxembourg (145), Germany (120) and the Netherlands (119), ahead of Austria and Belgium (113) and
well ahead of Denmark (106), France and Sweden (103).

### Where the real question is

The gap between Iceland's GDP index (128) and its consumption index (115) is the whole story: **the
income is high and so are the prices.** Iceland's price level for actual individual consumption is
**the highest in Europe** — above Switzerland, and 31% above Denmark, the most expensive EU member
state. On food it is 55.8% above the EU average, and higher than every EU member state by more than
33 points.

So the answerable version of the question is not "is purchasing power greater in the EU" — it is not
— but "**would membership lower Icelandic prices?**" That has an identifiable channel: the customs
union and the common agricultural policy would replace Iceland's national agricultural protection,
which this study's coverage map records as being higher than the CAP would allow, and food is where
Iceland's price gap is widest. **This study cannot quantify that effect to its confidence bar and
does not attempt to.** It is a real channel, not a demonstrated magnitude.

### What the study's own findings imply

The membership effect this project could identify is concentrated in the Eastern members: a median
gain of **+10.3 percentage points** relative to the US benchmark against a Western Balkan control,
with a confidence interval of [+5.1, +15.5] and a pre-accession placebo of +0.1. For Western
members, **no income effect is detectable at all**. Iceland is not a catch-up economy converging from
a low base; at 128% of the EU average it is at the top of the distribution. **On the study's own
evidence there is no basis for expecting membership to raise Icelandic purchasing power.** Nor, on
the same evidence, for expecting it to lower it.

## 5. Can an applicant negotiate terms that exclude fisheries or oil from EU control?

**Oil: it was never under EU control, so nothing needs excluding. Fisheries: no — and it is the one
resource in the Treaties where the answer is a hard no.**

### The negotiating principle

Accession negotiations settle the *timing and conditions* under which a candidate adopts the acquis,
not its content. The Council's negotiating framework — the standard text, quoted here from the
Ukraine framework of 25 June 2024 — states that "accession implies the acceptance of the rights and
obligations attached to the Union and its institutional framework, known as the 'acquis'", that the
candidate "will have to apply the acquis as it stands at the time of accession", and that
transitional measures are permitted only "provided they are **limited in time and scope**" and "must
not involve amendments to the rules or policies of the Union".

The structural point: **the negotiating framework contains no provision for permanent derogations at
all.** A permanent carve-out cannot be granted inside a negotiating chapter. It has to be written
into the accession treaty as a Protocol or an Article of the Act of Accession — primary law,
requiring unanimity and ratification by all 27 member states.

### Permanent carve-outs that have actually been granted

Five, and they are instructive precisely because of how narrow they are.

**Denmark, second homes** — Protocol No 32 to the Treaties, one sentence: "Notwithstanding the
provisions of the Treaties, Denmark may maintain the existing legislation on the acquisition of
second homes." From the 1973 accession, converted to a Protocol at Maastricht. No expiry.

**Malta, secondary residences** — Protocol No 6 to the 2003 Act of Accession. Malta retains its
permit regime on a non-discriminatory basis; an EU citizen may buy freely after five years'
continuous residence.

**Åland Islands** — Protocol No 2 to the 1994 Act of Accession. Finland may restrict acquisition of
real property, the right of establishment and the provision of services by persons without regional
citizenship; Åland is excluded from the territorial application of EU indirect tax harmonisation.
The closest thing in EU law to a permanent territorial carve-out — for a territory of about 30,000
people whose autonomy predates the Union by seventy years.

**Sweden, snus** — Article 151 of the 1994 Act of Accession. Its permanence is proved by the fact
that current law still writes around it: Article 17 of Directive 2014/40/EU prohibits oral tobacco
"without prejudice to Article 151 of the Act of Accession of Austria, Finland and Sweden". Note the
shape: Sweden may sell snus *in Sweden*, not export it into the single market.

**Finland and Sweden, northern agriculture** — Article 142 of the 1994 Act. Open-ended authority to
grant "long-term national aids" to maintain agriculture north of the 62nd parallel. But it is a
permission to spend national money inside a Commission-authorised envelope, not an exemption from
the common agricultural policy.

Everything else was a clock. Poland got 12 years on agricultural land, six other 2004 entrants seven
years, and four states five years on secondary residences. All expired; Poland's on 1 May 2016.

**None of the five is a sector. Four are about who may buy property, one is about a tobacco
product.**

### Fisheries is the hard case, and Iceland's own government has said so

Two facts settle it. First, **Article 3(1)(d) TFEU** makes "the conservation of marine biological
resources under the common fisheries policy" an **exclusive** Union competence — the only natural
resource on that list of five. Exclusive competence means member states may legislate only if
empowered by the Union. Second, the **principle of relative stability** allocates national quota
shares on the basis of historic catch. A new entrant does not negotiate a share of a future pie; it
is written into an allocation key derived from a reference period, and changing that key later
requires Council agreement in which its weight is proportional to its size.

Malta's 25-nautical-mile zone is the precedent usually cited and it does not carry the weight put on
it. Council Regulation (EC) No 813/2004 limits fishing in the zone to vessels under 12 metres not
using towed gear, with a capacity cap. The criteria are **vessel-based and apply to every EU vessel
alike** — which is exactly what makes it lawful. It gives Malta no quota-setting power, and because
it sits in a Council regulation rather than a Treaty Protocol it can be amended without Malta's
consent.

The most that has ever been conceded is **Article 49 of the 1994 Act of Accession**: Norway was
authorised "until 30 June 1998" to set exploitation rates for resources north of 62°N, "with the
exception of mackerel". Three and a half years, one sea area, mackerel excluded. It was a phase-out,
not a settlement — and the Norwegian referendum failed anyway, 52.2% against.

**Iceland's own negotiation makes the point most sharply of all.** Of the 35 chapters, 27 were
opened and 11 provisionally closed before the process stopped. **Chapter 13, fisheries, was never
opened.** Iceland never tabled a negotiating position, because the Commission never issued the
screening report that would have allowed the chapter to open. The Icelandic Ministry for Foreign
Affairs' own summary states flatly: "**The European Union alone holds the competence to conserve
resources through its common fisheries policy**", and identifies the difficult questions as "formal
authority over marine resources, limitations to investments and advocacy at international level".
The chapter that matters most to Iceland is the one that was never tested.

### Oil is a false worry

**Article 345 TFEU**: "The Treaties shall in no way prejudice the rules in Member States governing
the system of property ownership." **Article 194(2) TFEU** preserves a member state's right to
determine the conditions for exploiting its energy resources. And the Hydrocarbons Licensing
Directive 94/22/EC opens on the express premise that "Member States have sovereignty and sovereign
rights over hydrocarbon resources on their territories", with Article 2(1) providing that "Member
States retain the right to determine the areas within their territory to be made available" for
exploration and production. What the directive requires is that *when* a state opens an area, the
licensing procedure is transparent and non-discriminatory. It requires no state to open anything.

**Denmark proves the point.** A member since 1973, with its own North Sea production, a 20% state
share in all new licences through Nordsøfonden — and in December 2020 it unilaterally cancelled all
future licensing rounds and set a 2050 end date for existing licences. A member state shutting down
its own oil industry by national decision is the practical demonstration that Brussels does not own
it. Romania, a member since 2007, is the EU's largest natural gas producer.

### Where this leaves the Icelandic question

The dormant application is why "resuming" is legally coherent. Iceland applied in July 2009,
negotiations opened on 27 July 2010, the government put them on hold on 1 June 2013, and its letter
of 12 March 2015 requested that "Iceland should not be regarded as a candidate country for EU
membership". **Neither the Council nor the Commission characterises that letter as a withdrawal.**
The Council "took note" and made practical adjustments to its working procedures. There is a dormant
file, not a closed one.

And the substantive position is unchanged since 2013: **oil and geothermal resources are not at
risk; fisheries cannot be carved out; and the chapter that would decide it has never been opened.**

## What could not be established to the confidence bar

Stated so that nothing above is read as more certain than it is.

The **exact text of Article I-6 of the 2004 Constitutional Treaty**, widely reported to have
contained an express primacy clause, could not be retrieved from an official source; the claim that
Declaration 17 replaced it is therefore reported as context, not as verified fact. The **Storting
vote of 22 March 2018 is widely reported as 73 to 23** but the official tally page would not render;
the Supreme Court judgment separately records a related motion defeated 72 to 24, which is
consistent. **Whether HR-2023-2030-P was unanimous** is inferred, not read off an explicit statement.
The **Commission and the Council give different dates for Iceland's application** — 16 July 2009 and
17 July 2009 respectively — so neither is presented as uncontested. Whether the **12 March 2015
letter was legally a withdrawal** is a contested question this document does not settle; what is
established is that neither institution calls it one. All **Eurostat 2025 figures are provisional**
and will be revised in December 2026; single-point differences should be treated as noise. **Iceland's
2010 General Position** could not be read, because every Icelandic government domain returned HTTP
402 through the only available route; the Ministry's own retrospective summary is used instead.
Finally, the **magnitude of any price effect from adopting the CAP and the customs union** is not
estimated anywhere above, because this study has no specification that could estimate it to 95%.
