# What Iceland pays now that EU money would replace

Written 6 August 2026. The question: from public records, what is Iceland currently paying in
grants and support that EU funds would offset or replace on accession?

The short answer is that **one line dominates and it is smaller than the question assumes**,
that **two of the four candidate lines cannot be offset at all** — one because it is trivially
small, one because the money flows the opposite way — and that the largest single financial
consequence of accession is not on this list, because it is a payment out rather than a
payment in.

A warning that applies to everything below. The offset question has a clean answer on the
*paying* side, where Icelandic budget documents are public, and no answer on the *receiving*
side, because a new member state's envelopes for both the CAP and cohesion are **negotiated
figures written into an annex**, not outputs of a formula. There is no calculation that turns
Iceland's farm area into a direct-payments ceiling. Anyone who gives you a number for what
Iceland would receive has either invented it or is quoting an old negotiating estimate.

---

## What Iceland pays today

All figures against 2025 GDP of **4,956 bn ISK** and central government expenditure of
**1,605.8 bn ISK** (Statistics Iceland, provisional/preliminary).

| Line | Who pays it | bn ISK/yr | % of GDP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Farm support, budgetary transfers | the Treasury | **18.3** | 0.37% |
| Farm support, market price support | consumers, at the border | ~12.8 | 0.26% |
| **Total support to agriculture** | | **~31** | **0.63%** |
| Regional development | the Treasury | 0.7 | 0.01% |
| Fishing fee — revenue **to** the state | the fishing industry | **−17.3** | −0.35% |

**These two farm lines add; they are not a split of one number.** An earlier draft of this
table presented 16.3 and 12.8 as components "of which" of 18.3, which is arithmetically
impossible and was caught building the chart. The 18.3 bn is budgetary spending in 2024. The
12.8 bn is market price support in 2020, which sits *on top* of the budgetary leg, not inside
it. The 56/44 ratio quoted below is the split of the ~29 bn total on the 2020 figures
(16.3 budgetary + 12.8 market price support).

### Agriculture is the whole question

The *búvörusamningar* — the four agricultural agreements covering cattle, sheep, horticulture
and a general framework — were **extended by one year to the end of 2027** on 4 June 2026, with
a new framework due to take effect on 1 January 2028. The contracted total at fixed 2016 prices
declines gently from 13.78 bn ISK in 2017 to 12.66 bn in 2026; the nominal figure including
other support was about **18.3 bn ISK in 2024**, split roughly 41% cattle, 28% sheep, 7%
framework, 6% horticulture.

Iceland supports its farmers at one of the highest rates in the developed world. The OECD's
*Agricultural Policy Monitoring and Evaluation 2024* places Iceland in the top four of the 54
countries it covers, with support **above 40% of gross farm receipts** in 2021–23, alongside
Norway, Switzerland and Korea. The EU's comparable figure is **16.4%** (2022–24). Iceland's
Agricultural University puts the total support coefficient above 70% of the value of
agricultural production against roughly 25% for the EU, UK and US.

**The split between budgetary transfers and market price support is the hinge of the whole
question, and it is routinely missed.** Roughly 56% of Icelandic farm support is money paid out
of the state budget; the other 44% is delivered through border protection and paid by consumers
in higher food prices — a separate sum on top, not a slice of the budget line. Only the budgetary leg is a candidate for replacement by EU grants. The
market-price-support leg is not replaced by a grant at all — it is dissolved by the customs
union, and shows up as lower shop prices rather than as a transfer. Iceland's food price level
is 55.8% above the EU average, which is where that leg has been going.

### Regional development is a rounding error

Byggðastofnun's operating appropriation is about **212 m ISK** for 2026, the institute funds
itself largely from interest on its rural lending, and the visible *byggðaáætlun* grant lines
run to a few hundred million ISK a year. The whole area is **0.01% of GDP**. Whatever happens
to it on accession is not a material number.

### Fisheries runs the other way

Iceland pays no meaningful fisheries subsidy. The *veiðigjald* is a resource rent charge
levied **on** the industry: 19.5 bn ISK assessed and **17.3 bn ISK collected** for the 2026 fee
year, after the law of 1 November 2025 re-based pelagic valuations on Norwegian market prices
and cod and haddock on fish-market rather than related-party prices. Projected collection is
18–19 bn ISK a year to 2030.

**Note the symmetry.** The fishing fee collected and the farm support paid are almost the same
size — around 17–18 bn ISK each. Iceland taxes its internationally competitive resource sector
at roughly the rate at which it subsidises its uncompetitive one. Accession touches both, in
opposite directions and by different mechanisms.

### The EEA Grants obligation would genuinely end

Iceland is currently a **donor**, not a recipient. The EEA Financial Mechanism is **€1.805 bn
for 2021–2028**, funded by Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway and paid to 15 poorer EU member
states. (The separate Norway Grants, €1.463 bn, are 100% Norwegian and are not Iceland's
concern — a distinction the acquis map on Iceland's page previously got wrong.) This obligation
would end on accession and is a true offset.

**Iceland's own share of that mechanism is not published anywhere I could reach**, and the
commonly repeated figure of around 3% could not be confirmed. The best directly sourced
Icelandic number is old: a 2011 contribution of 1,255 m ISK and a ceiling of €33.975 m for the
whole 2009–2014 period. On that scale the obligation is single-digit millions of euros a year —
real, but an order of magnitude below the farm bill.

## What EU money would actually come back

### The CAP is the only large channel, and a new member enters at a quarter of it

Direct payments are not calculated from a member state's farm area. They are a **national
ceiling written into Annex V of Regulation (EU) 2021/2115**, historically derived and adjustable
only by delegated act. For an acceding state the ceiling is phased in on a schedule written into
the accession act. The Croatian precedent, from Article 121 of Regulation (EC) No 73/2009:

| 2013 | 2014 | 2015 | 2016 | 2017 | 2018 | 2019 | 2020 | 2021 | 2022 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25% | 30% | 35% | 40% | 50% | 60% | 70% | 80% | 90% | **100%** |

**Ten years from accession to the full rate, entering at one quarter.** The 2004 and 2007 waves
took nine years each. Whatever envelope Iceland negotiated, it would receive a fraction of it
for a decade — during which its own support obligations would not have shrunk.

The EU average is **€261 per eligible hectare** planned for financial year 2027, ranging from
€193 in Latvia to €487 in Greece. But the phrase carrying the weight is *eligible hectare*, and
for Iceland it is unresolved. Article 4 of the CAP Strategic Plans Regulation counts permanent
grassland, and Commission guidance confirms a member state **may** define permanent grassland to
include land that can be grazed even where grasses are not predominant — the hook through which
heath and rough grazing could enter the payment base. It is a member-state choice made in the
Strategic Plan and approved by the Commission, not an entitlement. For a country whose
cultivated area is small and whose rough grazing is vast, that single definitional choice would
move the answer by more than any other variable in this document, and it cannot be resolved from
outside a negotiation.

### Cohesion would pay Iceland almost nothing

This is the clearest finding here, and it goes against the intuition that joining brings
structural funds. Regions are categorised at NUTS 2 level by GDP per capita against the EU
average: less developed below 75%, transition 75–100%, more developed above 100% (Article 108,
Regulation (EU) 2021/1060). **Iceland is at 128%.** It would be a single more-developed region.

The Commission allocates more-developed regions at an aid intensity of about **€18 per head per
year**, against a realised average of roughly **€207 per head in less developed regions** — an
order of magnitude apart. The observable end of this is Luxembourg, whose entire seven-year
cohesion allocation under Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/1131 is **€29.8 million** —
about €4.3 m a year. Denmark receives €262 m over seven years; Ireland €909 m; the Netherlands
€925 m.

Iceland would also be excluded outright from the **Cohesion Fund**, whose eligibility gate is at
member-state level on GNI per capita and whose 15 eligible states are listed in an annex Iceland
would not be on.

### The northern-agriculture route is national money, not EU money

This is the precedent that actually fits, and it is the one most likely to be misread. **Article
142 of the 1994 Act of Accession** lets Finland and Sweden pay long-term national aid to
agriculture north of the 62nd parallel — and **all of Iceland is north of 62°N**. Finland pays
about **€293 million a year** under it, covering 55.5% of its agricultural area; Sweden about
**SEK 413 million**, covering 11%.

Read that carefully. Article 142 is not the EU paying Finland. It is the EU **permitting Finland
to keep paying its own farmers**, inside a Commission-authorised ceiling. The northern precedent
does not say "your national support is replaced by Brussels money." It says "you may continue a
substantial part of it out of your own budget." So the honest expectation is partial substitution
plus continued national spending, not a clean swap.

The genuinely EU-funded northern channel is **areas facing natural constraints** under Article 71
of Regulation (EU) 2021/2115, financed from rural development, whose mountain category expressly
includes areas north of the 62nd parallel. Observed rates run **€12 to €271 per hectare**. It is
optional — five member states do not operate it at all — and it is bounded by additional costs
and income foregone rather than by a rate card.

### Fisheries funding has no key for a new entrant

EMFAF's national allocations are frozen historical shares: each state receives the same
percentage of the fund it received in 2014–2020. **A new member state has no prior share, so
there is no formula to apply** — a key would have to be created in the negotiation. The fund is
small in any case (€6.1 bn for 2021–2027, of which €5.3 bn shared management, against €307 bn
for the CAP), and Article 13 makes vessel construction, acquisition and capacity increases
ineligible by default.

### The change of legal form, which is not a money question but matters

Even where national support continues, its **shape** would have to change. The Commission's state
aid guidelines for agriculture prohibit operating aid — support "granted solely on the basis of
price, quantity, unit of production or unit of the means of production." A scheme paid per litre
of milk or per head of livestock is incompatible and would have to be recast into permitted
categories. Iceland's agreements are structured substantially that way today. Top-ups layered
onto an approved rural development intervention escape state aid control entirely, which is the
channel Finland and Sweden use, but the money has to be re-plumbed to get there.

## The line that is missing from the question

Every line above is small next to the one nobody asked about. Gross national contributions to
the EU budget run between **0.87% and 1.00% of GNI** for the high-income member states in the
2026 budget — Denmark 0.87%, Germany 0.88%, Luxembourg 0.94%, Finland and the Netherlands 0.96%,
Ireland 1.00%. Applied to an economy of Iceland's size that range is **43 to 50 bn ISK a year**:
**two and a half times the entire agricultural support bill**, arriving in full from year one,
while direct payments phase in over a decade from 25%.

That arithmetic is a scale indicator, not a forecast — it applies an observed range for other
countries to Iceland's GDP, and Iceland's GNI is not its GDP. It is offered only to fix the order
of magnitude, because a discussion of offsets that stops at the receipts side is measuring the
smaller half.

## What could not be established to the confidence bar

**Iceland's precise OECD %PSE.** Verified only as *above 40% of gross farm receipts, 2021–23,
top four of 54 countries*. The per-country OECD source documents are robots-blocked on the only
available route. Two specific figures — 41.8% and 63% — were produced by PDF text extraction
during research and were confirmed **absent from the source**. They are recorded here so they
cannot leak back in from a later, less careful pass. **Do not use either.**

**The budgetary/market-price split** of 16.3 bn against 12.8 bn ISK is 2020 and from a secondary
source citing OECD data. The structure is right and the order of magnitude is right; the vintage
is six years old.

**Iceland's share of the EEA Financial Mechanism** is not published on any reachable page. The
figure of about 3% is widely repeated and is *not* confirmed here.

**Annual EU programme participation fees for Iceland** — no published total was found.

**The treatment of common and rough grazing as eligible hectares**, which is the largest single
unknown on the receipts side and is not resolvable outside a negotiation.

**Cohesion capping rules** in the adopted Annex XXVI of Regulation (EU) 2021/1060, where the
European Court of Auditors and Bruegel give conflicting GNI bands. The Luxembourg, Denmark,
Ireland and Netherlands allocations quoted above come from Implementing Decision 2021/1131 and do
not depend on resolving that conflict.

**Net budgetary positions on a basis comparable to the Germany figure of 0.47% of national income
a year** used elsewhere in this study. The Commission stopped publishing the operating budgetary
balance series after 2018, and the available substitutes use different definitions.
